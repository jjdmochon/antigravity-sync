**The catalytic and other sophisticated functions of biomolecules arise from the placement of many atoms in three-dimensional space. Here, we describe a generative artificial intelligence method for biopolymers, RFdiffusion3 (RFD3), which employs free atom diffusion to satisfy complex sets of atomic constraints more rapidly and accurately than previous methods across several challenges from enzyme design to RNA structure design. We demonstrate experimentally the power of the RFD3 pipeline for enzyme design by designing de novo phosphotriesterases with a complex active site architecture incorporating a carbamylated lysine coordinating two metal ions and zinc proteases specific for the transthyretin protein involved in amyloid diseases; in both cases we obtain active catalysts in zero shot 96 construct design campaigns.**

There has been considerable recent progress in de novo design of proteins with new functions using generative deep learning methods ( _1_ – _12_ ). Most methods, such as RFdiffusion (RFD1) ( _1_ ) and BindCraft ( _4_ ), represent proteins at the amino acid level. This granularity suffices for designing protein monomers, assemblies, and protein-binding proteins, but lacks the resolution to specify enzyme active sites and other functional sites at the atomic level, and detailed interactions with small molecules and nucleic acids. RFdiffusion2 (RFD2) ( _3_ ) addressed this challenge by enabling the conditioning of residue-level diffusion on a small number of side chain atoms critical for catalysis or ligand binding; however, because diffusion remains at the residue level, it is incapable of creating additional side chain interactions with ligands or catalytic residues. In structure prediction, AlphaFold3 (AF3) ( _13_ ) utilized a diffusion process over every individual atom conditioned on rich representations of protein geometry generated by a separate conditioning module. Further work ( _14_ , _15_ ) showed that atomic diffusion processes can generate protein backbones and can be extended to model side chains, but these efforts did not enable modeling of interactions with non-protein components or the design of functional sites described at the atomic level. Methods developed for RNA

and DNA design ( _16_ – _18_ ) have also been primarily at the residue level, making it difficult to scaffold functional sites in de novo designed ribozymes.

We hypothesized that a general solution to the challenge of designing sites with complex constellations of protein atoms interacting with small molecules, nucleic acids and other non-protein components could be achieved using atombased diffusion including both backbone and sidechain atoms while conditioning on the non-protein components of the system and the positions of key protein atoms contributing to function. We reasoned further that such a system could be readily extended to biopolymers more generally, enabling the generation of functional proteins and RNAs in a single unified framework. We set out to develop such a model for biopolymer function design based on atomic level descriptions of catalytic and other functional sites (Fig. 1, A and B).

## **RFdiffusion3 architecture and training**

residue-frame based representations of RFdiffusion2 and RFdiffusion2-MI ( _19_ ), which have enabled the design of high activity enzymes ( _20_ , _21_ ) but can require very large numbers of trajectories to satisfy complex catalytic constraints in silico. Interactions across the Protein Data Bank (PDB) span proteins, small molecules, RNA and DNA, and units of these interactions are inherently atomic. Thus, we set out to develop an atomic-level generative approach, rather than one based on amino acid residues.

A first challenge in using such an approach for protein and nucleic acid design is how to atomically represent side chains and bases when the sequence is unknown, since amino acids and bases have different numbers of side chain atoms. To address this issue, we treat all protein residues as having 4 backbone atoms and 10 side chain atoms ( _22_ ) (the largest canonical sidechain, tryptophan, has 10 atoms) (fig. S1), supplementing the smaller sidechains with additional virtual atoms placed on the Cβ. For the nucleic acid phosphate-sugar backbone there are 12 backbone atoms for RNA and 11 for DNA, and 11 total atoms for the bases (the largest bases have 11 atoms; fig. S2), for at most 23 atoms for the general nucleic acid plus protein case. For isosteric residues such as cysteine and serine, and bases C and U, we assign distinct virtual atom indices as padding atoms, enabling the model to differentiate between them despite their identical bonded geometry.

To generate atomic coordinates starting from random initializations, we adopted a transformer-based U-Net architecture, similarly to the AF3 diffusion module, with three parts: (i) a downsampling module that encodes atomicand residue-level features including the partially noised structure, (ii) a sparse transformer module that processes token-wise information, and (iii) an upsampling module that modulates the atomic features with the token-wise features and predicts coordinate updates (Fig. 1C) ( _13_ , _23_ ) (see the “RFdiffusion3 architecture and inference” section of the materials and methods for further details of architectural modifications from AF3 diffusion module). First, information transfer is conducted through a series of sparse attention blocks between atomic and token features, only allowing residues and atoms that are geometrically close in the noise to attend to each other. These distance-based keys encourage the network to focus on local interactions, help prevent overfitting and mitigate artifacts observed with cropping. Second, following prior work ( _24_ ), we use a cross attention mechanism to up-pool and down-pool between the finegrained atom representations and coarser-grained token representations. In these updates, the features of each atom are weighted by an attention operation before they are pooled into token features, enabling the token-level transformer to focus on specific atoms within each token. After processing, the token features are read out from the token track using a cross attention by token splitting.

Protein design differs from protein structure prediction in that the input is not an amino acid sequence but instead a set of high-level design conditions (protein length, possible target molecules, etc). Whereas AF3 uses the time- and compute-intensive Pairformer module to extract distance and other information from the input amino acid sequence, for design we reasoned that a much lighter weight module could suffice for processing conditioning information. We thus shrunk the Pairformer from 48 to just 2 layers, greatly reducing computational cost, and ultimately yielding only 168M trainable parameters for the final network (cf. ~350M for AF3). To further improve generation efficiency (Fig. 1D), we omit computationally expensive triangle multiplicative and triangle attention updates from the network, widely used in prior architectures for protein design ( _1_ – _3_ , _14_ , _22_ ) and structure prediction ( _2_ , _13_ , _25_ , _26_ ). The processed conditioning information is input into the modified diffusion module described above, where most of the compute time is spent. Inspired by the AlphaFold recycling mechanism in the Pairformer, we introduce recycling at each diffusion step to allow additional computation during inference without increasing the number of trainable parameters. The natively all-atom architecture of RFD3 considerably simplifies the specification of atomiclevel constraints compared to RFD2 ( _3_ ), providing precise control over hydrogen bonding, catalytic constraints, atomic level burial profiles, ligand interactions and other atomic level features without requiring specification of the location in the sequence of the residues contributing to the scaffolded site (fig. S2). For enzyme active site design, binder design and motif scaffolding challenges, the coordinates of the motif or binding partner are provided within the developing noise cloud, allowing the network to refine the structure in the context of the motif. We found that Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) ( _27_ ) improved adherence conditioning information (fig. S2). Overall, this ensures that the generated biomolecules respect the precise geometry of the given functional motifs.

We trained on all complexes in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) ( _28_ , _29_ ) including protein-protein, protein-small molecule, nucleic acids, protein-NA and RNA-small molecule interfaces. We include an array of design tasks, including motif scaffolding functional motifs (table S1), enabled by the AtomWorks dataloading framework ( _30_ ). Additionally, we supplement the training set with high quality AlphaFold2 (AF2) ( _31_ ) distillation structures predicted as described in ( _32_ ) and RosettaFold3-predicted RNA structures ( _30_ ) for nucleic acid design. Training examples are generated by noising protein and nucleic acid structures to different extents and predicting the positions of the backbone and side

chain atoms for a given residue provided some subset of the applicable conditioning information; including features such as hydrogen bonds, binding partners, or functional motifs to enable conditioning on these properties during inference. Since nucleic acid structures are heavily driven by basepairing, we implemented token level base pair conditioning to constrain the model to produce unpaired and paired bases as specified (Fig. 2A). Given the range of tasks for training RFD3, we adopt a hierarchical training procedure to prevent overfitting. We pre-train the network on a mix of AF2 predictions and the structures deposited in the PDB across all protein design tasks, and then fine-tune on a larger fraction of DNA binding, RNA design and protein-protein interaction tasks. We also produce an RNA-tuned checkpoint for use on RNA specific tasks. When training across the PDB, we use entries deposited through December 2024. Full details of the architecture and training procedures are provided in the supplementary methods.

RFD3 generates protein and nucleic acid sequences along with the structure, closely resembling the natural distributions (fig. S1). We found in initial tests with unconditional protein generation that the consistency of the sequence with the structure (as assessed using AF3 or RF3) could be improved by redesign using ProteinMPNN ( _33_ ). Therefore, in all the protein design tasks, we used ProteinMPNN (or LigandMPNN ( _34_ ) for challenges with nonprotein atoms) to generate sequences after completing the diffusion trajectory. In contrast, we found that the RNA sequences generated by the model were foldable and thus were directly used for RNA design challenges. As shown in fig. S1C, for unconditional design, the expected diversity and designability of the model are tunable using the step scale parameter. In the following sections, we compare RFD3 to other structure generators within commonly used pipelines using ProteinMPNN and structure prediction networks which are known to enrich designs for experimental success ( _1_ , _8_ , _11_ , _20_ , _35_ – _38_ ).

For protein binder evaluation, we generated 200 designs against 5 targets, assigned sequences with ProteinMPNN, repredicted the structures using RF3, and assigned in silico success for structures that meet confidence and structural cutoffs that correlate with experimental success. We find that RFD3 outperforms RFD1 on all five benchmark cases and outperforms the contemporaneously released BoltzGen model in three of the five cases (fig. S3). RFD3 produces highly diverse structures, exceeding the diversity of RFD1 and BoltzGen on all five tasks, despite being more than twice faster for typical length proteins (Fig. 1D). We also find that the tradeoff between designability and diversity can be modulated in RFD3 by adjusting inference-time settings, including those affecting the temperature of sampling (step scale; c.f fig. S1C) and an anti-loop conditioning flag (fig. S3).

We benchmarked RFD3 on small-molecule binder design using a set of four molecules introduced in ( _2_ ) and assess the fraction of designs that are predicted with high confidence. We find RFD3 outperforms the other diffusion based methods (RFdiffusion-AllAtom and Boltzgen) in three of the four cases in the fraction of structures correctly recapitulated with AF3 (figs. S4 and S5). The ability to condition generation based on the desired atom-level solvent accessibility of the target molecule enables guidance of the location of the molecule relative to the protein center of mass enabling finergrained guidance of the orientation of the molecule (fig. S4), as well as the overall center-of-mass via pre-specification of the origin coordinates (fig. S4). For protein-nucleic acids interactions, we experimentally characterize sequence specific DNA binding proteins using RFD3 in concurrent work ( _39_ ). We expect considerable synergies between RFD3 and the recently described structure prediction-MPNN cycling methods ProteinHunter ( _11_ ) and HalluDesign ( _12_ ) for binder design. # **Enzyme design with RFdiffusion3**

Designing de novo enzymes requires precisely arranging specific atoms to catalyze a reaction ( _21_ , _44_ – _47_ ). The backbone coordinates and sequence positions of catalytic residues are generally not specified in advance, so a method that jointly infers the scaffold structure, catalytic side-chain rotamers, and sequence positions from minimal atomic constraints is desirable ( _3_ ). Previously, we showed RFD2 can create scaffolds around active sites by diffusing the active site at atomic resolution and the rest of the protein at residue level and training the backbone atoms of the active site to overlap with a residue backbone in the protein (Fig. 3B). The model then diffuses the corresponding backbone in context of the active site, while simultaneously determining the corresponding rotamer and sequence position for each catalytic residue.

We expected that the fully atomistic representation of RFD3 would reduce the complexity of generating scaffolds around minimal atomic motifs representing the catalytic residues. To scaffold unindexed atomic motifs with RFD3, we append an extra token for every catalytic residue to the diffused tokens, allowing the network to process the active site constraints in context (Fig. 3B). As opposed to RFD2, where the full catalytic residue had to be represented to identify backbone positions (Fig. 3B, top), in RFD3, the atomic motif conditioning tokens only correspond to the atoms that are being fixed in the atom-level transformer (Fig. 3B, bottom). The model is trained to overlap a diffused protein or RNA atom with each atom in the provided atomic motif (Fig. 3A).

We compared RFD3 to RFD2 on a set of atomic motif enzyme (AME) scaffolding problems from the Mechanism and Catalytic Site Atlas (M-CSA) ( _48_ ). On the AME benchmark, the difficulty of problems typically increases as the number of catalytic motif islands increases. We find RFD3

outperforms RFD2 on 37 out of 41 (90%) of such tasks, achieving improved pass rates on harder problems (Fig. 3D and fig. S6). To further elucidate this, we benchmarked the methods on active sites from four enzymes that each contain 6 catalytic residues, an aldolase, ornithine decarboxylase, glycosidase and a phosphotriesterase (details of active site construction in the “Active site preparation and configuration” section of the materials and methods), providing a closer representation of design-scale accuracy requirements of the model. We found in all four cases RFD3 was able to generate more designs where the active site residues were recapitulated after refolding to within 1.25 Å. The quality of scaffolded catalytic residues was measured using a rotamer probability loss calculated based on backbone-dependent Dunbrack rotamer probabilities ( _49_ ), where a loss of <1 represents more native-like rotamers (computation detailed further in the “Rotamer probability loss” section of the materials and methods). We find RFD3 consistently generates higher quality rotamers in comparison to RFD2 (Fig. 3E, additional data in fig. S6). A representative example of the improvement in performance is shown for the glycosidase case in Fig. 3C; RFD3 achieves more precise placement of the catalytic glutamates and other catalytic residues around the sugar substrate. Motivated by this observation, we hypothesized that RFD3 could be extended beyond single-chain motif scaffolding to generate symmetric protein complexes by explicitly enforcing symmetry during the generative process. We evaluated this capability using a subset of 15 cases from the AME benchmark that exhibit native C2 symmetry (fig. S7). The method performs robustly even in challenging cases involving up to seven catalytic residues per subunit (fig. Across all 15 cases, RFD3 produces physically plausible symmetric designs that satisfy both structural fidelity and symmetry requirements, demonstrating that symmetry-aware diffusion can effectively generalize atomic motif scaffolding to multimeric enzyme design.

As RFD3 can generate both nucleic acid and protein structures, the atomic level motif scaffolding can be applied to complex active sites with catalytic contributions from both RNA and protein residues. As a model, we chose the RNA cleaving active site from RNAse P, which employs both amino acid sidechains and RNA bases to mediate activation of a

catalytic water to attack the substrate scissile bond. As shown in Fig. 3G, RFD3 readily generates hybrid RNA/protein assemblies that closely recapitulate both the sidechain and nucleobase contributions to the catalytic site. This ability to readily combine diverse modalities should enable the application of RFD3 to a wide range of frontier problems in protein design.

# **De novo phosphotriesterase design**

As a testament to the complexity of de novo enzymes the RFD3 pipeline is capable of generating, we sought to zeroshot design active phosphotriesterases for organophosphate hydrolysis. Organophosphates are widely used as insecticides and industrial additives like flame retardants, yet many are environmentally persistent ( _50_ ) and acutely toxic because they irreversibly inhibit serine hydrolases such as acetylcholinesterase, the mechanism exploited in their use as nerve agents ( _51_ ). Native enzymes only hydrolyze a small subset of organophosphates, so designed enzymes that neutralize these compounds have broad value for environmental remediation and medical countermeasures ( _52_ – _54_ ). Native phosphotriesterases feature six catalytic residues that coordinate two metal ions, including a posttranslationally carbamylated lysine that bridges the metals ( _55_ ). Because each catalytic residue lies in a separate motif island and organophosphates are usually small and flexible, this active site is substantially more complex than those of previously designed de novo enzymes ( _20_ , _21_ ).

We chose paraoxon as a model substrate, a flexible organophosphate that releases a yellow product upon hydrolysis (Fig. 4A). We used quantum chemistry to model the transition state for hydroxide attack on paraoxon in the phosphotriesterase active site ( _56_ ) and enumerated histidine coordination modes, side chain flips, and general base identities to create input theozymes for RFD3 (Fig. 4B). We used RFD3 to generate scaffolds for each theozyme variant, using RASA-conditioning to bury the hydrophobic ethyl groups of paraoxon while exposing the nitrophenol leaving group to solvent. Following sequence design with LigandMPNN, designs for which AF3 ( _13_ ) and Chai-1 ( _57_ ) predictions closely recapitulated the target active site geometry were selected for experimental characterization.

We expressed 96 designs in _E. coli_ with a C-terminal Strep-tag II and purified them by Strep-Tactin affinity chromatography. The purified proteins were supplemented with ZnSO4 and screened for the hydrolysis of paraoxon by monitoring for the nitrophenol product absorbance at 405 nm (fig. S11A). We identified 4 active designs, all derived from the same base RFD3-generated scaffold. Single clones of these hits were isolated, sequence-verified, and produced at scale for kinetic characterization. Hypothesizing that the designs would be dependent on the catalytic lysine carbamylation for

activity (Fig. 4D), we supplemented the purified proteins with NaHCO3 which interconverts to CO2 to drive the carbamylation of phosphotriesterase ( _58_ ). The most active design, identified from well D1 and named Zinc-Activated Paraoxon Phosphotriesterase 1 (ZAPP-1), performs multiple turnover catalysis (fig. S8) and had a _k_ cat = 0.015 ± 0.003 s<sup>−1</sup> and _k_ cat/ _K_ M = 2.0 ± 0.6 M<sup>−1</sup> s<sup>−1</sup> (Fig. 4, C to E; figs. S8 to S10; and table S2). ZAPP-1 is a predominantly helical bundle with a loop positioned over the active site that may function as a lid, and it shows near-perfect agreement with the AF3 prediction of the post-translationally modified protein-metalsubstrate complex, with a Cα RMSD of 0.53 Å.

To test whether ZAPP-1 catalyzes the phosphotriesterase reaction as designed, we performed kinetic analysis without bicarbonate supplementation after purification and observed an 11-fold decrease in _k_ cat and a 4-fold decrease in _k_ cat/ _K_ M (Fig. 4E). This suggests that the purified protein was not fully carbamylated and that restoring this modification is important for activity. Furthermore, we treated ZAPP-1 with triethyloxonium tetrafluoroborate to ethylate and stabilize the lysine carbamate, enabling its detection by electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (fig. S11) ( _59_ ). We then generated knockouts of all six catalytic residues and expressed each mutant from a single colony at scale for kinetic characterization (Fig. 4F, fig. S9, and table S3). All catalytic knockouts had complete or substantial losses in activity. Together, these results suggest that the designed active site mediates the observed ZAPP-1 phosphotriesterase activity.

To demonstrate the reproducibility of our RFD3 design pipeline, we performed a second design campaign using a theozyme derived from a machine-learning force field study of ZAPP-1 (fig. S12) ( _60_ ) and experimentally screened 192 designs (Fig. 4G). We identified multiple active hits and characterized the top 7, spanning 4 distinct scaffolds (Fig. 4, H and I, and fig. S13). These designs showed kinetics comparable to ZAPP-1, with ZAPP-2 achieving increased _k_ cat = 0.023 ± 0.006 s<sup>−1</sup> , _k_ cat/ _K_ M = 4 ± 3 M<sup>−1</sup> s<sup>−1</sup> , and a rate enhancement _k_ cat/ _k_ uncat of (5 ± 2) × 10<sup>5</sup> (fig. S14). ZAPP-1 through ZAPP-5 exhibit phosphotriesterase activity across distinct scaffolds and catalytic residue arrangements, highlighting the diverse structural solutions that RFD3 generates for the same catalytic problem. The ability to scaffold complex active sites featuring multiple cofactors, post-translational modifications, flexible substrates, and multiple catalytic residues is a major advance in enzyme design that will enable the computational design of more sophisticated biocatalysts.

# **De novo design of proteases cleaving transthyretin**

We next assessed the capability of the RFD3 pipeline to design enzymes acting on biologically relevant

macromolecule substrates. We designed proteases targeting human transthyretin (TTR; UniProt P02766), whose misfolding and aggregation are associated with systemic TTR amyloidosis, leading to polyneuropathy, cardiomyopathy and renal failure ( _61_ , _62_ ). The sequence forming the longest β- strand in the core of the TTR fibril [PDB ID: 8GBR, ( _63_ )], PAINVAVHVF, was chosen as the target epitope (Fig. 4J). The theozyme geometry was defined by merging catalytic residues taken from aminopeptidase N (PDB ID: 4QHP) with substrate backbone atoms at positions P1 and P1′ and adjacent stabilizing protease backbone atoms extracted from the AF3 prediction of the astacin-substrate complex (Fig. 4K; see supplementary materials for details). RFD3 was used to simultaneously scaffold the catalytic residues, determine the substrate conformation and generate a customized active site cleft to accommodate the prescribed substrate sequence.

We generated 96 designed enzymes and tested activity in cis by fusing them to the substrate peptide followed by mScarlet. 56/96 designs exhibited >10% cleavage after incubation at 37°C for 18 hours as measured by SDS-PAGE analysis (fig. S15). The active designs were subsequently tested in trans using a fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) assay based on an sfGFP-peptide-mScarlet reporter construct where the best hits, TTRg4, TTRg10 and TTRg11, were selected. Of the three, TTRg11 (Fig. 4L) cleaved the majority of the substrate in 1 hour (fig. S18, C to E) as confirmed by SDS-PAGE gel analysis (Fig. 4M). Mass spectrometry further verified cleavage at the designed site (fig. S15). To investigate the substrate sequence specificity of TTRg11, we measured the activity of TTRg11 against its cognate substrate as well as six additional substrate sequences from proteins involved in a range of β- amyloidogenic diseases ( _64_ – _67_ ). Although these sequences all exhibit substantial β-strand propensity, TTRg11 exhibited much higher activity on its cognate substrate than toward the alternative substrates (Fig. 4N).

We further confirmed the Zn(II) dependence of TTRg11 (Fig. 4O) and determined its kinetic parameters. As a oneshot designed protease, without any experimental optimization, TTRg11 exhibited a _k_ cat of 0.072 ± 0.008 s<sup>−1</sup> , a _K_ M of 70 ± 10 μM and a _k_ cat/ _K_ M of 1100 ± 300 M<sup>−1</sup> s<sup>−1</sup> (Fig. 4P, fig. S16, and table S4). The kinetic parameters of this designed protease are within the range (although at the low end) of native proteases with well-characterized biological functions and broad applications ( _68_ – _70_ ). These results are a considerable advance over protease design with RFD2 ( _3_ , _19_ , _71_ ) both in the very high success rate in cis (56 out of 96 designs with activity) and in achieving activity on natural peptide derived substrates rather than synthetic substrates optimized for cleavability using two sided design calculations.

# **Conclusion**

The RFD3 pipeline enables precise scaffolding of constellations of atoms in proteins, nucleic acids, and protein-nucleic acid hybrids for a wide range of biomolecular functions. The accuracy and versatility of RFD3 have several origins. First, atom-level design constraints are most naturally implemented in a model where the atoms are the fundamental unit, rather than residues. For RFD2 ( _3_ ), the specified catalytic atoms were essentially a new data type, as all other components are residues; in contrast, in RFD3, the catalytic atoms are identical to all other tokens except that their coordinates are fixed (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, in RFD2, general atom-level conditioning on hydrogen bond donor and acceptor status and atomic burial cannot be carried out since only a small subset of atoms are represented explicitly. Second, while 14 atom per protein residue approaches have been experimented with ( _22_ , _35_ ), this was done in the context of architectures more similar to the AF3 pairformer and so were more compute intensive, and no approaches were implemented for providing the conditioning information critical to designing proteins with new functions. Thus, RFD3 provides a more expressive model for design while achieving faster inference (Fig. 1D). The unified 23 atom RFD3 model enables atom level motif scaffolding of both nucleic acid and protein functional sites, which we highlight here with the exemplary design of RNAse-P like hybrid RNA/protein catalysts (Fig. 3G). The versatility and broad applicability of the method are demonstrated by our extensive in silico and experimental tests and those by the broader scientific community since the public release of the code ( _72_ , _73_ ). The control that can now be achieved in the design of complex functions with the RFD3 pipeline is exemplified in our results: for example, an enzyme active site can be specified by providing coordinates for a subset of substrate/transition-state atoms and the surrounding sidechain atoms; the hydrogen bonding donor/acceptor status of others; the extent of burial of individual sidechain

The control that can now be achieved in the design of complex functions with the RFD3 pipeline is exemplified in our results: for example, an enzyme active site can be specified by providing coordinates for a subset of substrate/transition-state atoms and the surrounding sidechain atoms; the hydrogen bonding donor/acceptor status of others; the extent of burial of individual sidechain and substrate atoms; and the overall placement of the substrate and active site relative to the protein center of mass. Experimental characterization of the phosphotriesterases and proteases highlights the ability of the RFD3 pipeline to generate enzymes that were challenging to design with previous structure-generation methods. We anticipate RFD3 will be broadly useful for a wide range of biomolecule design challenges and applications.
